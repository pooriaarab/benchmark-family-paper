The defect counts in Section~\ref{sec:review} rest on review records written after the defects were found. To test one claim before seeing outcomes, we registered a study on Zenodo on 2026-10-16, before the first run cell.\footnote{The record lives under concept DOI \texttt{10.5281/zenodo.23424272}: version \texttt{1.0} is the plan alone (\texttt{10.5281/zenodo.23424273}), version \texttt{1.1} added the deviation log (\texttt{10.5281/zenodo.23424493}), and the current version \texttt{1.2} is \texttt{10.5281/zenodo.23424604}.} Appendix~\ref{app:deviations} logs every change made before any real-model cell finished.

\paragraph{The registered design.}
The benchmark checklists this paper cites ask for a trivial agent: a policy that ignores the task. The registered study plays \trivialPolicies{} such policies against the first \trivialPackages{} packages at versions 0.1.0 and 0.2.0: \trivialCells{} cells, \trivialFailures{} failures. Each policy answers through the package's own provider path, the same path a real model uses, and the score is the package's own mean; Appendix~\ref{app:experiments} names the policies, the local server and the runner.

\paragraph{H1 is not supported.}
The registered primary hypothesis says the best trivial-policy score $T(p,v)$ is higher at 0.1.0 than at 0.2.0 in most packages. Table~\ref{tab:trivial} shows what happened, and Figure~\ref{fig:trivial-scatter} in Appendix~\ref{app:results} plots the same cells. $T$ was higher at 0.1.0 in \trivialHigherOld{} packages: checkout-consent fell from \expTrivCheckoutConsentOld{} to \expTrivCheckoutConsentNew{}, and tool-errors from \expTrivToolErrorsOld{} to \expTrivToolErrorsNew{}. It was higher at 0.2.0 in \trivialHigherNew{} package (ranking-stability rose from \expTrivRankingStabilityOld{} to \expTrivRankingStabilityNew{}) and tied in \trivialTied{}. A one-sided exact sign test gives $p = \trivialSignP{}$ on $n = \trivialSignN{}$ non-tied packages. The registered smallest effect of interest was a drop in $T$ of a tenth of a point or more in at least half of the packages; only checkout-consent and tool-errors reach it, so H1 is not met. The result is a null: the generic policies caught the logged defects in only \trivialHigherOld{} of \trivialPackages{} packages.

\paragraph{What the null does and does not show.}
The blind-strategy holes the probing reviewers logged in pelican, honesty, mcp-safety and sycophancy needed task-aware attacks: a memorised SVG earns credit only when it is the drawing the task asks for, and honesty's abstention value is a constant only a reader of the package would know to send. A generic policy never plays either. The \trivialHigherOld{} packages where generic policies did catch a defect paid credit to a generic output shape: checkout-consent gave a never-pay trace partial credit, and tool-errors paid an empty submission. So the checklist advice to run constant or empty policies does not by itself catch the defect class this suite is built on. With $n = \trivialSignN{}$ non-tied packages, though, the sign test has almost no power: the null bounds what the registered design can claim, and it does not measure what generic baselines are worth in general. The suite does not claim they catch nothing; it reports that they caught \trivialHigherOld{} of \trivialPackages{} here.

\paragraph{The extended battery.}
The same \expPolicies{} policies then ran on every pinned artifact in the suite: \expArtifacts{} artifacts covering all \expPackages{} packages, \expCells{} cells and \expFailedCells{} failures (Appendix~\ref{app:experiments} holds the version pins and the seed rule). The wave-1 cells reproduce the registered table cell for cell. The wave-2 arm is exploratory and sits outside the registration: those packages have only a 0.1.0 release, and some of their changelogs already record fixes. At the released version of each package the best blind-policy score is \expTrivialReleasedMax{}, on \expTrivialReleasedMaxPkgs{}; \expTrivialReleasedZero{} of \expPackages{} released graders give every blind policy zero. The \expTrivialReleasedNonzero{} released packages with nonzero blind credit are instruction-hierarchy (\expTrivInstructionHierarchyRel{}), payment-delegation (\expTrivPaymentDelegationRel{}), ranking-stability (\expTrivRankingStabilityNew{}) and rollback-recovery (\expTrivRollbackRecoveryRel{}). All four are findings on the pinned artifacts: a policy that never reads the task still earns credit there. \expWaveTwoNonzero{} of the four sit in the wave-2 arm, outside the registration and with no second release to compare against; the ranking-stability cell is part of the registered grid at the released 0.2.0. Headroom, the mock oracle mean minus the best trivial mean, is \expHeadroomMedian{} at the median package; the smallest is \expHeadroomMin{} on \expOracleBelowOnePkgs{}, whose packaged oracle abstains by design (Appendix~\ref{app:validation}), so its ceiling sits lower for reasons unrelated to grading quality.

\paragraph{Where blind policies die.}
Figure~\ref{fig:trivial-reasons} in Appendix~\ref{app:results} sorts the \expZeroRows{} zero-score rows into the frozen reason classes. \expFormatGateZeroPct{} percent die at the format gate, rejected before substantive scoring; the rest reach the grader and lose there (\expClassWrongAnswer{} graded wrong answers, \expClassContractViolation{} contract violations, \expClassAbort{} aborts, \expClassUnclassified{} unclassified). Per package the dominant gate differs: \expFormatGateAllPkgs{} reject every blind row at the format gate, while \expFormatGateNone{} packages never show a format-gate row. Among the latter, apology-pizza, sycophancy and test-weakening score every blind submission zero inside the grader. The mechanism behind the null is therefore twofold: where a submission contract exists, a policy that cannot read the task usually fails to produce a parseable answer at all, and where the submission reaches the grader it earns nothing because the credit sits behind task-specific behaviour.
