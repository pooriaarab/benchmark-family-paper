\title{Agents Can Build Benchmarks. Approvals Missed What Probing Found: 22 Cases}

\begin{abstract}
Coding agents built a suite of \suitePackages{} benchmark packages holding \suiteTasks{} tasks on one shared engine, and the public record shows \councilMergedApproved{} of the \councilMergedFeature{} merged feature pull requests carried an LLM approval with no test job failing at a merge commit. Probing the shipped packages still found defects that needed a fix round in every one of them (median \reviewRoundsMedian{}, maximum \reviewRoundsMax{}), across \defectClasses{} defect classes, and at least \defectBlindStrategyCredit{} packages paid credit to a strategy that ignored the task. Re-running the logged exploits on the published tarballs replicates \expExplReplicated{} of \expExplRecordClauses{} record clauses, and the median attack score falls from \expExplAtkMedianOld{} before the fixes to \expExplAtkMedianNew{} after. The check the benchmark literature prescribes caught almost none of it: under the preregistered design the best of \trivialPolicies{} generic blind policies scored higher before the fixes in only \trivialHigherOld{} of \trivialPackages{} packages ($p = \trivialSignP{}$), and the best blind score on any released grader is \expTrivialReleasedMax{}. The difference is review depth: approvals read diffs while these defects lived in graders and held-out splits that only running the artifact exposes. We release the engine, the packages, the probing protocol, the defect record and the scripts behind every number, and we condense the classes into five checks to run before an agent-built benchmark ships. No real model was run and no leaderboard is kept; the registered real-model arm is unfinished.
\end{abstract}

\impact{A published benchmark can be gamed twice: once by a model that overfits the public tasks, and once by a reader who treats a high score as a certification. Several packages are about refusal, consent, or not leaking a secret; a high score says nothing about what to buy or whether a deployment is safe. Checkout-consent uses a fake shop and a test payment and must not be pointed at a real card; mcp-safety uses a canary string and must not be pointed at a production tool server holding real credentials. The released engine, packages, re-review protocol, review log and defect counts with their public sources let the checks in this paper be repeated and extended. This release reports no real-model results and keeps no leaderboard.}
